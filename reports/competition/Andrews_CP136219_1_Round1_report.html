Below is a beginner-friendly walkthrough of the main decision→outcome links, with Capsim terms explained the first time they appear. We will name specific mistakes clearly when they matter — always with the goal of helping you recover, not discouraging you.

Quick glossary used below: An emergency loan is automatic short-term borrowing Capsim forces on you when cash would otherwise go negative — it is expensive and a signal that financing and cash planning did not match spending. Contribution margin (CM) is the percent of each sales dollar left after variable production costs (material, labor, inventory carrying) — before SG&A, depreciation, and interest. Awareness is the share of customers who know your product exists (driven mainly by promotion budget). Accessibility is how easy it is for customers to find and buy your product in the sales channel (driven mainly by sales budget and how many products share that budget). A fine cut is the “sweet spot” circle on the perceptual map for a segment; customers strongly prefer products inside it. MTBF (Mean Time Between Failure) is reliability in hours; each segment has a required band.

  1. 1. Cash stayed under your control (no emergency loan). Ending cash $6,289, net profit $1,281, operations cash flow $2,858. You issued stock $0 and LTD $0, with plant improvements of $0. Keep this discipline — it is a real competitive advantage in Capsim.
  2. 2. Acre (Low End) was wanted — and you stocked out. You sold about 1,795 units against potential demand closer to 1,945. A stockout means customers were ready to buy and you did not have enough product on the shelf — that is a supply/scheduling opportunity, not a “customers dislike us” story. Accessibility (how easy it is to find and buy you) was 33.0%. Next round: raise the production schedule (and check capacity) so you capture the demand you already earned.
  3. 3. Contribution margin needs care. Company CM was 25.2% with SG&A/Sales at 11.2%. Thin CM means even decent sales struggle to cover overhead. Automation on Low End, cleaner product offers, and marketing spend matched to products that can actually sell will help Round 2.
  4. 4. Marketing reach still has runway. Awareness (share of customers who know you exist — driven mainly by promotion budget) and accessibility (ease of finding/buying you — driven by sales budget) vary across your lineup. Compare each product to segment leaders in the boxes below.
The through-line for Andrews: stockouts on products customers already wanted, and profits you can build on. None of this is permanent. Round 2 is recoverable with clearer cash planning, production matched to real demand, and focused R&D/marketing on the products closest to what customers want.

What to Focus on Next Round

Priorities tailored to Andrews — concrete problems first, then stretch. These are focus areas, not a full decision set.

1 Protect cash discipline

You avoided an emergency loan (cash $6,289). Keep it that way. Any new plant/automation spend should be paired with explicit financing or clear operating cash. Do not retire long-term debt early if liquidity might tighten.

2 Capture demand on Acre

You stocked out on product(s) customers already wanted. Raise the production schedule (and evaluate capacity if utilization is already >100%), and check accessibility so sales budgets support the extra volume.

3 Tighten R&D and marketing on soft products

Focus revisions on Able (Traditional), Adam (High End), Aft (Performance): move toward each segment’s ideal spot, keep MTBF inside the band, and fund awareness/accessibility enough to compete with leaders.

4 Competitors to watch

Study Garrett, Erie, Harper — not to copy blindly, but to see how they paired financing with investment, and which segments they defended on price vs positioning.

You can do this. Competition Round 1 is graded, and it is also the first real data point in a multi-round story. Teams that read their numbers carefully, fix one or two concrete offer mistakes, and plan cash deliberately often rebound hard in Round 2. Andrews: take the priorities above one at a time — progress beats perfection.
