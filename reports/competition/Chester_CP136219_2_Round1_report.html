Below is a beginner-friendly walkthrough of the main decision→outcome links, with Capsim terms explained the first time they appear. We will name specific mistakes clearly when they matter — always with the goal of helping you recover, not discouraging you.

Quick glossary used below: An emergency loan is automatic short-term borrowing Capsim forces on you when cash would otherwise go negative — it is expensive and a signal that financing and cash planning did not match spending. Contribution margin (CM) is the percent of each sales dollar left after variable production costs (material, labor, inventory carrying) — before SG&A, depreciation, and interest. Awareness is the share of customers who know your product exists (driven mainly by promotion budget). Accessibility is how easy it is for customers to find and buy your product in the sales channel (driven mainly by sales budget and how many products share that budget). A fine cut is the “sweet spot” circle on the perceptual map for a segment; customers strongly prefer products inside it. MTBF (Mean Time Between Failure) is reliability in hours; each segment has a required band.

  2. 2. Cake (Traditional) was wanted — and you stocked out. You sold about 1,241 units against potential demand closer to 1,278. A stockout means customers were ready to buy and you did not have enough product on the shelf — that is a supply/scheduling opportunity, not a “customers dislike us” story. Accessibility (how easy it is to find and buy you) was 61.0%. Next round: raise the production schedule (and check capacity) so you capture the demand you already earned.
  3. 3. Cedar showed real traction in Low End. Roughly 17.2% segment share on about 2,013 units sold. Protect what is working — keep the offer inside the fine cut, fund awareness/accessibility enough to hold shelf presence, and make sure production can meet demand so a good product does not stock out.
  4. 4. Coat showed real traction in Performance. Roughly 16.5% segment share on about 443 units sold. What this means for next round

Capability building only works if the commercial offer and cash plan are coherent. For Chester: add production/capacity on stocked-out products before heavy new automation elsewhere. You have time — Round 2 is where disciplined teams separate from hopeful ones.

What to Focus on Next Round

Priorities tailored to Chester — concrete problems first, then stretch. These are focus areas, not a full decision set.

1 Protect cash discipline

You avoided an emergency loan (cash $14,546). Keep it that way. Any new plant/automation spend should be paired with explicit financing or clear operating cash. Do not retire long-term debt early if liquidity might tighten.

2 Capture demand on Cake

You stocked out on product(s) customers already wanted. Raise the production schedule (and evaluate capacity if utilization is already >100%), and check accessibility so sales budgets support the extra volume.

3 Competitors to watch

Study Digby, Garrett, Andrews — not to copy blindly, but to see how they paired financing with investment, and which segments they defended on price vs positioning.

You can do this. Competition Round 1 is graded, and it is also the first real data point in a multi-round story. Teams that read their numbers carefully, fix one or two concrete offer mistakes, and plan cash deliberately often rebound hard in Round 2. Chester: take the priorities above one at a time — progress beats perfection.
